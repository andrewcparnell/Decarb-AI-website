Welcome

What is Dragon’s Den?

A BBC television format, known as Shark Tank in the US, in which entrepreneurs pitch a business to a panel of investors and then face their questions.

On television

Founders have a few minutes to win over the dragons, who probe every weak spot before offering money for equity.

Our version

You work in pairs to create a spin-out company built on real intellectual property, then pitch it to a panel of judges.

Why we do it

Commercial thinking makes your PhD stronger. You will use these skills for impact statements, funding bids and real spin-outs.

Before next week

Watch an episode or two if you have never seen the programme. It is the fastest way to hear what a good pitch sounds like.

Your task

Create a spin-out company with viable IP

  • You will work in assigned pairs for the three final weeks of the training scheme
  • Invent something: the company must rest on intellectual property you can defend
  • The theme should fit broadly within the remit of Decarb-AI, which is pleasantly wide
  • Everything is due by 27 November, when you pitch to the judges
  • Remind yourself of our research areas at www.decarb-ai.org

Choosing an idea

What makes an idea worth pitching

A real problem. Someone is losing time, money or sleep over it today
A named customer. Not “farmers” but “dairy co-ops with more than 200 suppliers”
Something you own. A method, model or dataset that others cannot simply copy
A climate angle. Emissions avoided, resilience gained, or resources saved
A believable first step. Something a small team could build within a year
Honesty about risk. Judges trust teams who name their weakest assumption
Room to choose. Generate ten ideas as a pair, then kill nine of them quickly
Something you care about. Three weeks is short, but it is long enough to be bored
Deliverable one

The business canvas

One page, nine boxes. It forces you to describe a whole business without writing a thirty-page plan, and it shows the judges at a glance that you have thought it through.

  • Developed by Osterwalder and used across industry and technology transfer offices
  • Expect to redraw it three or four times. The early versions are meant to be wrong
  • Template: ie.cam.ac.uk/business-model-canvas-template.pdf

Deliverable one

The nine boxes

01Key partnersWho do you depend on? Universities, data providers, cloud, distributors, certification bodies.
02Key activitiesWhat must your company do well every day? Modelling, validation, support.
03Key resourcesPeople, data, compute, and above all the IP itself.
04Value propositionThe promise at the centre. What problem do you solve, for whom, and how much better than today? If this box is vague, nothing else saves you.
05Customer relationshipsSelf-service tool, consultancy, long-term contract, or something else.
06ChannelsHow does your product reach the customer? Direct, through agencies, or embedded in their software.
07Customer segmentsWho pays? Be specific and name the first ten customers you would call.
08Cost structureSalaries, compute, data licences, certification. What dominates?
09Revenue streamsSubscription, licence, per-report, per-hectare, or a share of savings. How much, from whom, how often?

Template: ie.cam.ac.uk/business-model-canvas-template.pdf

Deliverable one

Filling it in well

  • Start on the right. Customer and value proposition first, then work back to activities and costs
  • Use numbers. “Saves roughly €40k a year per site” beats “delivers major savings”
  • Mark your assumptions. Circle the one that, if wrong, sinks the company
  • Test it cheaply. Two phone calls or emails to a real potential customer will teach you more than a week of reading

A quick sanity check

Can you say, in one sentence: we help X achieve Y by Z?

If the sentence needs an “and also”, you are describing two companies.

Deliverable two

The Invention Disclosure Form

A short confidential document that records an invention inside the university. It is the first formal step in every spin-out and every licence, and it starts the clock on protecting your idea.

  • It tells the technology transfer office what you have invented and why it is new
  • It establishes who contributed, which matters later for ownership and reward
  • It is confidential, so you can describe the invention fully and honestly
  • Submit a short IP Identification Form to Dr Steve Donoghue by 20 November, and he runs it through NovaUCD’s Tradespace AI
  • You need at least one completed form. If your idea contains two separable inventions, write two

UCD guidance: ucd.ie/innovation/knowledge-transfer/researcher-supports/tell-us-about-your-idea

Deliverable two

Intellectual property in one slide

Patents

Protect a new, inventive and useful technical method. Strong, slow and expensive. Software and models can be patentable when they produce a technical effect.

Trade secrets

Protect what you never reveal: a training recipe, a calibration, a pipeline. Free, but gone the moment it leaks or someone works it out.

Copyright

Automatic for code, text and figures. Protects the expression, not the underlying idea, so it rarely stops a competitor on its own.

Data & know-how

Often the real asset. A labelled dataset, a validated benchmark, or a relationship with the people who hold the data.

Public disclosure before filing can destroy patentability. Talk to NovaUCD before the preprint, poster or talk
Ask early. The office would far rather hear a half-formed idea than a published one
Deliverable two

What goes in the form

The invention. What is it, in plain language that a non-specialist can follow?
The problem. What is done today, and why is that unsatisfactory?
The novelty. What is genuinely different from the nearest published work or product?
The stage. Idea, simulation, prototype, or tested with real data?
The contributors. Who did the inventive work, and under whose funding?
The disclosures. Has any of it been shared publicly, and when?

Deliverable three

Delivering it well

  • Rehearse out loud, with a timer. Three times at least. Silent reading always underestimates
  • Share the stage. Agree who takes which slides and who fields which questions
  • One idea per slide, in text large enough to read from the back of the room
  • Drop the jargon. If a word needs a definition, it is costing you a sentence you need
  • Answer the question asked. A short honest “we don’t know yet, here is how we would find out” scores well
  • Keep spare slides after the end for the questions you expect
Common mistakes

What loses the room

Nine minutes of method and one minute of business. Starting with the model instead of the problem. No named customer. Running over time.

Be ready

Questions the dragons will ask

Prepare a one sentence answer to each of these before you present. The questions are where most teams win or lose.

“Who exactly writes you the first cheque, and why this year?”
“What stops a large technology company doing this next month?”
“Which part is genuinely novel, and which part is already published?”
“How big is the market in Ireland, and does this travel abroad?”
“What will the money be spent on, and what will it prove?”
“What would have to be true for this to fail?”
